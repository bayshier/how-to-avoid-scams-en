## #188 · "Your package was lost — we'll refund you double!"? The full play of precision-targeted refund scams and screen-sharing theft

`Privacy · Data Black Market` `🔓Privacy leak` `🎯Shop owners`

"What you bought last week, what you paid, your tracking number — he knows it better than you do."
This isn't a friend checking your delivery for you. It's a scammer. One three-minute phone call, and tens of thousands of yuan vanish from your card...
Last week, a woman got a call: "Hello, your package was lost in transit. We will process double compensation." She was skeptical — until the caller recited the exact product name, payment amount, delivery address, and even the tracking number without a single error. Once trust was built, the chain of traps began.
Today we take the upgraded "precision refund + screen sharing" scam apart in full. 💸

**🕵️ Deep breakdown: the three steps of precision refunds and covert card-skimming**

1️⃣ Privacy leak: the black industry behind your "transparent" data 📈
How did they know what you bought? Between the merchant shipping your order and your signature, your delivery data passes through many hands: photographed shipping labels, industry insiders selling it off, system vulnerabilities leaking... One piece of precise shopping data may sell on the black market for mere cents — yet it is the brick that pries open the door of your trust.
2️⃣ The upgrade: from "phishing links" to "meeting apps" 🪤
Scammers used to send links and have you type your bank card number and verification code into a fake official webpage. The playbook has now been fully upgraded: they no longer send links. Citing "remote guidance through the claims process," they ask you to download a meeting app (Tencent Meeting, Zoom, and the like) and turn on "screen sharing."
3️⃣ Remote monitoring: passwords and codes captured in the dark 💰
Once screen sharing is on, your phone screen is "transparent" to the scammer. As they guide you into your banking app and you type your payment password, they watch every keystroke from their side; even the SMS verification code they can intercept in real time off your screen. You may never hear a transfer notification before your entire savings have been swept away.

> 🛡️ Avoid the pit — the four things to do the moment a "lost package / product quality compensation" call arrives:
> 🚫 Real platforms handle refunds only inside their own app: every legitimate e-commerce or logistics platform processes refunds within the official app. They never make unsolicited private calls, and they never ask you to add WeChat or QQ.
> 🔒 Refuse unofficial links and sensitive requests: any request to click an SMS link, enter your bank card number, or provide a phone verification code is 100% fraud.
> 🚨 Never enable "screen sharing": any request to download a meeting app and share your screen for "guided operation" means hang up immediately.
> 💡 Everyday privacy protection: tear off or black out the shipping label before discarding boxes; use a parcel locker or a privacy number as the delivery address.
The internet is full of routines, and scammers never run out. Follow this channel to stay protected.
🛑 Hang up on such calls, then open the official shopping app yourself and check your order and refund status with real customer service. Three extra minutes of verification can protect years of savings.

## #308 · Extreme warning: how many steps from stolen to sold? The data black market's "four-step harvest chain" and the five industries full of insiders

`Privacy · Data Black Market` `🔓Privacy leak`

"Hello, is this the owner of vehicle XX? Your plate number is XX and your car insurance expires next week..." 📞
You get these calls all the time. The caller knows your name, your address, what you drive — even where your child goes to school and which hospital you were treated at. They know you better than you know yourself.
In the big-data era, how exactly does our privacy leak? From stolen to sold, a piece of personal data crosses just four steps on the black market — and the insiders who sell us out are hiding in the five industries we use most. 👇

**🪤 The core secret: the data black market's "four-step harvest chain"**

The data black industry strikes with such precision because it runs a highly polished closed loop of circulation and monetization:
Step 1: Insiders strike 💻 — the colleague at the next desk, the parcel-station worker, a company manager: they slip in a USB drive unnoticed, or use an internal account to bulk-copy customer names, phone numbers, ID numbers, policy numbers, or shipping-label data.
Step 2: Data circulates 🌐 — through encrypted chat apps or social software, the stolen data is dumped cheap to middlemen. A courier record: ¥0.5. A car-insurance record: ¥0.2. Volume discounts available.
Step 3: Layered resale 🗂️ — middlemen clean, organize, and classify the first-hand data, then resell it to ever more downstream buyers: renovation companies, loan shops, fraud rings... layer by layer, wider and wider, the data is squeezed endlessly.
Step 4: Precision bombardment 🎯 — the final buyer, holding your complete file, starts dialing like crazy. Because they hold all your cards, the lies they tell sound seamless — and are almost impossible to guard against.

**👤 Privacy-leak disaster zones: who exactly are the insiders?**

Who is selling out our trust? In recent years, the five worst industries for violating citizens' personal information:
1️⃣ The courier industry 📦
Some couriers, chasing fat profits, bribe warehouse staff who hold label-reprint permissions: type in an order number, hit reprint, and even a signed-for package spits out a complete label — name, phone, address, everything. Harder crews rent internal accounts outright for a few hundred yuan a day and work in shifts to bulk-export as many as 400,000 courier records, dumped wholesale at ¥1 apiece.
2️⃣ Insurance companies 🚗
The telematics sales head of a well-known insurer privately packaged the province's vehicle-owner data city by city and sent it downstream — and his buyers were fellow sales managers. 247,000 records sold for ¥231,000, under ¥1 per record. The bitterest irony: the buying manager pushed the expense through his company's normal approval flow and booked it as "advertising and courier fees." Part of the premium you pay becomes the budget for buying your privacy.
3️⃣ Real-estate agencies 🏢
Some agents used high-privilege internal accounts to pull property owners' phone numbers; a single record fetched ¥100–500 on the black market, and one person alone illegally made ¥3 million reselling. Another salesperson sold 35,000 owner records to a dozen renovation companies — owners bought a home and got renovation-spam calls every day after.
4️⃣ Medical institutions 🏥
An operations team leader at a medical institution never touched data by day. Every night after ten o'clock — sometimes in the small hours — he remoted into his employer's system from home and copied VIP clients' names, phone numbers, and treatment items one by one. He kept it up for six full years: over 30,000 records resold, illegal gains over ¥200,000.
5️⃣ Telecom carriers (the most terrifying: location tracking) 📍
Some carrier engineers abused their positions to query cell-tower locations. Enter a phone number, and out come coordinates, map points, and timestamps with error under one kilometer. Query the same number repeatedly, and a person's daily movements and travel routes can be reconstructed in full.

> 🛡️ Final word: how do insiders get caught — and how do we protect ourselves?
Heaven is watching, and so are system logs. These insiders were caught through anomalous IPs, late-night logins, and spikes in customer complaints. An account exporting 30,000 records in one night, or a system perpetually remote-logging in from a residential block at 3 a.m., trips risk controls automatically — and what awaits them is the law's severe punishment and real prison sentences.
Billions of stolen identity records circulate on global data markets. When data this precise falls into fraudsters' hands, it becomes impersonating Chinese police/prosecutors/courts (fake arrest warrants delivered over FaceTime/video calls), "loan account cancellation" calls, or romance-investment "pig-butchering" scams (the standard English term for romance-then-investment fraud) — every one of them lethal.
Having your privacy sold is not your fault, but protecting yourself is your right.
In daily life, be sure to:
Tear off or black out courier labels; never toss them intact 📝
Be careful downloading apps from unknown sources; turn off location, contacts, and other non-essential permissions 📱
When a stranger opens with your full name and core private details, don't panic — hang up and report 🛑
Your privacy should never be the cheapest bargaining chip on the data black market.
The internet is full of routines, and scammers never run out. Follow this channel to stay protected.
